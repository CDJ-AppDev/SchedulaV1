**Schedula: Build Your Daily Blocks**

**1.1 PROJECT CONTEXT**

Time management is one of the most consistently studied metrics of student and professional performance. A systematic review by Patzak et al. (2025) uncovered that structured behavior such as planning, prioritization, and task organization in higher education and in the workplace significantly improve productivity, wellbeing, and performance even in high-pressure environments and demanding programs. When working with teams, Roels and Corbett (2024) found that organizations give little thought when deciding when or how often groups meet for their tasks, as coordination and planning itself tends to consume time instead of allotting it to doing the actual work. This brings light to a need for individuals to structure, manage and visualize their time, as well as for groups to have deliberate rules to coordinate with each other without wasting time. This is where Schedula enters the picture, offering a solution to give a plotted visualization while giving teams a shared structure.

One notable topic in this field is the University Course Timetabling Problem (UCTP), which Abdipoor et al. (2023) describes as a formally recognizable operations research problem that tackles the growing number of students, sections, rooms and other constraints, eventually too fast for manual solutions to keep up. Ceschia et al. (2023) confirms this predicament as it remains as an active and unresolved area of study, showing a need for methods that balance a schedule that works with a schedule that is good. These studies confirm securing conflict-free and preference-aware schedules is a significant problem that needs computational power to be solved.

When trying to uncover a solution, a unanimous problem emerges, manual ad-hoc scheduling does not scale up to growing needs. In the UCTP, manually structured timetables are not guaranteed an optimal outcome once factors reach a certain threshold. Chen et al. (2022) raises another concern where even when a schedule has no conflicts it still fails to account for other human factors, such as personal preferences and workload balance.

In addition, successfully coordinating availability across multiple people is another recognizable problem in itself. Roels and Corbett (2024) states that the right way to decide schedules heavily depends on team size and how team member workloads vary. Their study found that small teams benefit from flexible coordination, while larger groups need stricter scheduling rules to avoid wasting time. This affirms the need for a team-based scheduling tool built for departments, student organizations, and classes which provides a platform with mechanisms that make group coordination efficient. 

To achieve a solution, many literature point to shared calendars as a functional requirement for optimal coordination. Roels and Corbett (2024) established that fixed interval structures work best for large teams because it clearly dictates when meetings should happen, which can be executed efficiently on visible and shared schedules. Moreover,. Tran et al. (2023) describes that a system that lets team members see and reconcile with each other's preferences have higher satisfaction outcomes. When applied to scheduling, this determines that a centralized team schedule is better than constructing schedules blindly. Another aspect of having shared calendars emphasizes the value of knowing everyone’s free time instead of just knowing each other’s existing commitments. Roels and Corbett (2024) states calling for a meeting only functions well when you can see when team members are actually free for one. Without knowing each other’s availability, teams are vulnerable to interrupting each other, especially in a large corporate-like setting. 

This paves the way to the concept of Collaborative Scheduling, which we ought to describe how to extend single-user calendar tools into shared decision making systems. Tran et al. (2023) discussed in their review of “consensus models” how consensus-building is conceptually how we can achieve smart suggestions or conflict resolutions features in a scheduling tool, awaiting integration into a well-designed system.

To finish our foundation of what makes a good schedule, we can separate factors into two categories: non-negotiables that ensure feasibility without conflicts, and soft constraints that will improve satisfaction but can be easily traded off if necessary. Chen et al. (2022) emphasizes the importance of considering student and instructor preferences into what is necessarily a good schedule as it measurably improves satisfaction. Therefore, Schedula will make use of these findings to achieve a balanced but practical system.

Among other systems, many personal productivity and auto-scheduling applications have the feature to organize calendars based on their user preferences, but they rarely help groups of people share and reconcile schedules. A study by Swain et al. (2023) reviewing Microsoft Viva Insights' Focus Time finds that, while the application increased self-reported performance and focus, it operates entirely within one calendar only, rather than coordinating around what the project needs for the team. Zhao et al. (2023) evaluated Procolli, a time-management app for university students, and found that while it reduced procrastination for some students, it had no mechanism for study groups to see each other’s availability and negotiate time together. Bowler et al. (2022) also examined modern digital calendars, and found that their interfaces leave little to no room to express uncertainty, tentative availability, and negotiation that group scheduling requires. 

Schedula proposes a system that provides organization, computational support, shared calendars, visible availability, and preference-awareness that reduces the manual time needed for workflows to determine coordinations.

**1.2 PURPOSE AND DESCRIPTION**

Schedula will be built as a web-application, providing a collaborative-scheduling environment for multiple users. Tran et al. (2023) states that consensus-based systems are typically deployed on accessible and always-on platforms, for members to view and respond to at any time.

The application will have a centralized and team-based schedule management environment which addresses the problem identified by Roels and Corbett (2024), avoiding wasted time on unclear structures scattered across multiple channels. A central interface for teams removes the need for manual updating and sharing of information that may easily escalate to confusion. 

In addition, having smart suggestions for possible compromises helps the system to move beyond detecting conflicts and dive into proposing negotiable adjustments based on the group’s availability and preferences. This feature maps to the research reviewed by Tran et al. (2023), which categorizes several mechanisms that can be implemented into Schedula’s logic.

**1.3 STATEMENT OF THE PROBLEM**

It is clear that manual and traditional scheduling practices are not sustainable as variables such as people, courses, and other constraints increase over time. These manual methods cannot reliably guarantee a conflict-free schedule, let alone factor-in individual preferences and availability. Even when a workable schedule is produced, there is no system to keep it updated, recheck for uncertain commitments, and resolve any potential compromises that may arise upon consolidation. This points to three problems that Schedula is designed to address:

1. How can schedule conflicts on availability and preference be detected and avoided automatically?  
2. How can a person or a team organize and manage multiple overlapping schedule combinations without losing track?  
3. How can users visualize and customize their schedules with more ease and convenience than existing manual or individual-based tools?
